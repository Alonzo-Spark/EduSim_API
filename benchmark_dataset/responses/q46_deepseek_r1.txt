### Ohm's Law: Core Principles and Limitations  

#### ⚡ Quick Definition  
Ohm's Law states that the electric current (\(I\)) flowing through a conductor is **directly proportional** to the voltage (\(V\)) applied across it and **inversely proportional** to its resistance (\(R\)), provided physical conditions (e.g., temperature) remain constant.  

---

#### 📜 Statement of Ohm's Law  
> **"The current through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage across the two points and inversely proportional to the resistance between them."**

#### ⚠️ Limitations of Ohm's Law  
Ohm's Law is **not universal**. It fails for components where resistance varies with external factors:  

| **Scenario**               | **Behavior**                                                                 | **Examples**                          |  
|----------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------|  
| **Non-Ohmic Materials**    | Resistance changes with voltage/current; \(V\) vs. \(I\) graph is non-linear | Diodes, Transistors, Semiconductors   |  
| **Temperature Changes**    | Heating alters resistance (e.g., filament bulbs)                            | Incandescent bulbs, Thermistors       |  
| **High Voltage/Current**  | Excessive energy breaks down material properties                            | Insulators under breakdown voltage    |  
| **AC at High Frequency**   | Skin effect, capacitance, inductance dominate                               | RF circuits, Transmission lines       |  

---

#### 🔑 Key Characteristics of Ohmic vs. Non-Ohmic Conductors  
| **Property**           | **Ohmic Conductors**                  | **Non-Ohmic Conductors**             |  
|------------------------|---------------------------------------|---------------------------------------|  
| **\(V-I\) Graph**      | Straight line through origin          | Curve (e.g., exponential, parabolic)  |  
| **Resistance**         | Constant (\(R = \frac{V}{I}\))        | Variable (depends on \(V\), \(I\))    |  
| **Examples**           | Copper, Aluminum, Resistors           | LEDs, Transistors, Vacuum tubes       |  

---

#### 💡 Applications  
- **Circuit Design:** Calculate voltage, current, or resistance in DC circuits.  
- **Power Management:** Determine power dissipation (\(P = I^2 R\)).  
- **Sensor Calibration:** Standardize resistive sensors (e.g., strain gauges).  

---

#### ⚖️ Advantages & Disadvantages  
| **Advantages**                     | **Disadvantages**                          |  
|------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|  
| Simplicity for DC analysis         | Only valid for ohmic materials            |  
| Foundation for circuit laws (e.g., Kirchhoff) | Fails under dynamic conditions (AC, temp shifts) |  

---

#### 📝 Important Notes  
1. **Ohmic Materials:** Metals obey Ohm's Law only if temperature is constant.  
2. **Non-Linearity:** Semiconductors exhibit exponential \(V-I\) relationships (e.g., Shockley diode equation).  
3. **Dynamic Resistance:** Defined as \(r = \frac{dV}{dI}\), not \(V/I\).  

---

#### 🔍 Summary  
- **Core Principle:** \(V = IR\) for ohmic conductors under stable conditions.  
- **Fails When:** Resistance depends on \(V\), \(I\), temperature, or frequency.  
- **Real-World Use:** Primarily for idealized resistors and DC analysis.
