┌───────────────────────────┐  
│      Quick Definition     │  
│                           │  
│ The **Work-Energy Theorem** states that the net work done on an object is equal to the change in its kinetic energy. │  
└───────────────────────────┘  

---

### Key Concept

The work-energy theorem provides a powerful link between **force**, **motion**, and **energy**. Instead of analyzing motion through Newton's laws and kinematics, this theorem allows us to understand how energy changes due to work performed by forces.

---

┌───────────────────────────┐  
│      Main Formula         │  
│                           │  
│   $$                      │  
│   W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K = K_f - K_i  $$  
│                           │  
│   where                   │  
│   - $W_{\text{net}}$: Net work done on the object  │  
│   - $\Delta K$: Change in kinetic energy         │  
│   - $K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$: Kinetic energy         │

### Derivation of the Work-Energy Theorem

We begin with the definition of **work** done by a net force and combine it with **Newton’s Second Law** and **kinematic equations**.

#### Step 1: Work Done by a Constant Net Force

For a constant net force $ F_{\text{net}} $ acting along a straight line, the work done over a displacement $ d $ is:

$$
W = F_{\text{net}} \cdot d
$$

From **Newton’s Second Law**:

$$
F_{\text{net}} = ma
$$

Substitute into the work equation:

$$
W = mad
$$

#### Step 2: Use Kinematic Equation (Without Time)

We use the kinematic equation that relates initial velocity $ v_i $, final velocity $ v_f $, acceleration $ a $, and displacement $ d $:

$$
v_f^2 = v_i^2 + 2ad
$$

Solve for $ ad $:

$$
ad = \frac{v_f^2 - v_i^2}{2}
$$

Now substitute into the work expression:

$$
W = m \cdot ad = m \cdot \left( \frac{v_f^2 - v_i^2}{2} \right)
$$

$$
W = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_i^2
$$

#### Final Result:

$$
W_{\text{net}} = K_f - K_i = \Delta K
$$

✅ Thus, the **net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy**.

---

### Important Notes

- This derivation assumed a **constant net force** and **straight-line motion**, but the theorem holds **even for variable forces** and **curved paths** when using calculus.
- For variable forces, work is calculated as:
  $$
  W_{\text{net}} = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} F_{\text{net}}(x)\, dx
  $$
  Combining with $ F = ma $ and chain rule leads to the same result: $ W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K $.

---

### Applications

- Calculating speed of an object after a force acts over a distance.
- Analyzing motion on frictionless surfaces.
- Solving problems in mechanics without needing time or acceleration explicitly.
- Foundation for conservation of mechanical energy (when only conservative forces act).

---

### Advantages

- Avoids vector analysis of forces at every instant.
- Simplifies problem-solving in dynamics.
- Works well with variable forces via integration.

### Disadvantages

- Does **not** give directional information or time details.
- Cannot determine trajectories directly.
- Only accounts for kinetic energy — other forms (potential, thermal) must be handled separately.
