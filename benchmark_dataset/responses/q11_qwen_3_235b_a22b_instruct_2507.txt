    <h2>Introduction</h2>
    <p>
      Sir Isaac Newton's three laws of motion form the foundation of classical mechanics. These laws describe the relationship between a body and the forces acting upon it, and its motion in response to those forces. They were first published in 1687 in Newton’s work *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*.
    </p>
  </div>

  <!-- Definition Card -->
  <div class="section-card definition">
    <h3>Quick Definition</h3>
    <p>
      Newton’s Laws of Motion are three fundamental principles that describe how objects move under the influence of forces. They apply to all macroscopic objects moving at speeds much less than the speed of light.
    </p>
  </div>

  <!-- Newton's First Law -->
  <div class="section-card">
    <h2>1. Newton’s First Law: Law of Inertia</h2>
    <p>
      <strong>Statement:</strong> An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion continues in motion with constant velocity unless acted upon by a net external force.
    </p>

    <div class="formula-card">
      <strong>Main Concept</strong>
      <div class="formula-display">
        If $$\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = 0$$ then $$\vec{v} = \text{constant}$$
      </div>
      <div class="formula-actions">
        <button>Explain Concept</button>
        <button>Open in Physics Lab</button>
      </div>
    </div>

    <p><strong>Explanation:</strong> This law introduces the concept of <em>inertia</em> — the tendency of an object to resist changes in its state of motion. No net force means no acceleration.</p>

    <p><strong>Example:</strong> A book lying on a table remains at rest until someone pushes it. A hockey puck slides across ice and eventually stops due to friction — if friction were absent, it would keep moving forever.</p>
  </div>

  <!-- Newton's Second Law -->
  <div class="section-card">
    <h2>2. Newton’s Second Law: Law of Acceleration</h2>
    <p>
      <strong>Statement:</strong> The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass. The direction of acceleration is in the direction of the net force.
    </p>

    <div class="formula-card">
      <strong>Main Formula</strong>
      <div class="formula-display">
        $$ \vec{F}_{\text{net}} = m \vec{a} $$
      </div>
      <div class="formula-actions">
        <button>Explain Formula</button>
        <button>Open Formula Lab</button>
      </div>
    </div>

    <p><strong>Variables:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>\( \vec{F}_{\text{net}} \)</strong>: Net force (vector, in newtons, N)</li>
      <li><strong>\( m \)</strong>: Mass of the object (kg)</li>
      <li><strong>\( \vec{a} \)</strong>: Acceleration (m/s²)</li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Explanation:</strong> This is the most famous of Newton’s laws and is quantitative. It tells us how much an object will accelerate when a force is applied.</p>

    <p><strong>Example:</strong> Pushing a shopping cart — the harder you push (greater force), the faster it accelerates. A heavier cart (more mass) accelerates less for the same push.</p>
  </div>

  <!-- Newton's Third Law -->
  <div class="section-card">
    <h2>3. Newton’s Third Law: Action and Reaction</h2>
    <p>
      <strong>Statement:</strong> For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.
    </p>

    <div class="formula-card">
      <strong>Principle</strong>
      <div class="formula-display">
        $$ \vec{F}_{A \to B} = -\vec{F}_{B \to A} $$
      </div>
      <div class="formula-actions">
        <button>Explain Principle</button>
        <button>Visualize Interaction</button>
      </div>
    </div>

    <p><strong>Explanation:</strong> Forces always occur in pairs. If object A exerts a force on object B, then object B exerts an equal and opposite force on object A.</p>

    <p><strong>Example:</strong> When walking, your foot pushes backward on the ground (action), and the ground pushes forward on your foot (reaction), propelling you forward. A rocket expels gas downward (action), and the gas pushes the rocket upward (reaction).</p>
  </div>

  <!-- Important Notes -->
  <div class="section-card notes">
    <h3>Important Notes</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Newton’s laws apply in <strong>inertial reference frames</strong> — frames that are not accelerating.</li>
      <li>The second law is a vector equation: force and acceleration have direction.</li>
      <li>Action and reaction forces act on <em>different</em> objects — they do not cancel each other.</li>
      <li>These laws break down at speeds close to the speed of light (use relativity) or at atomic scales (use quantum mechanics).</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
