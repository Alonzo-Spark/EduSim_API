### Understanding Resistors in Series

This section will explore how resistors behave when connected in a series circuit.

---

### Quick Definition

A **series circuit** is a circuit arrangement where components are connected end-to-end, forming a single path for the electric current to flow.

---

### Key Concepts

*   **Current (I):** In a series circuit, the current flowing through each resistor is the same. This is because there is only one path for the charge to travel.
*   **Voltage (V):** The total voltage supplied by the source is divided among the resistors in the series circuit. The sum of the voltage drops across individual resistors equals the total voltage across the combination.
*   **Ohm's Law:** This fundamental law states the relationship between voltage, current, and resistance: $V = IR$.

---

### Derivation of Equivalent Resistance

Let's consider a circuit with three resistors, $R_1$, $R_2$, and $R_3$, connected in series to a voltage source $V$.

**1. Current in Series**

Since there is only one path for the current, the current flowing through each resistor is the same as the total current from the source.

$$
I_{total} = I_1 = I_2 = I_3 = I
$$

**2. Voltage Division**

According to Kirchhoff's Voltage Law, the total voltage across the series combination is the sum of the voltage drops across each individual resistor.

$$
V_{total} = V_1 + V_2 + V_3
$$

**3. Applying Ohm's Law**

Using Ohm's Law ($V = IR$) for each resistor:

*   For $R_1$: $V_1 = I R_1$
*   For $R_2$: $V_2 = I R_2$
*   For $R_3$: $V_3 = I R_3$

**4. Substituting into Voltage Equation**

Substitute these expressions for $V_1$, $V_2$, and $V_3$ into the total voltage equation:

$$
V_{total} = I R_1 + I R_2 + I R_3
$$

**5. Factoring out Current**

Factor out the common current $I$:

$$
V_{total} = I (R_1 + R_2 + R_3)
$$

**6. Defining Equivalent Resistance**

If we replace the series combination of $R_1$, $R_2$, and $R_3$ with a single equivalent resistor, $R_{eq}$, that draws the same total current $I$ from the same total voltage $V_{total}$, then from Ohm's Law:

$$
V_{total} = I R_{eq}
$$

**7. Final Expression**

Comparing the two expressions for $V_{total}$:

$$
I R_{eq} = I (R_1 + R_2 + R_3)
$$

Dividing both sides by $I$ (assuming $I \neq 0$):

---

### Main Formula

$$
R_{eq} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3
$$

*   **Explain Formula:** This formula states that the equivalent resistance of resistors connected in series is simply the sum of their individual resistances.

---

### Generalization

For $n$ resistors connected in series, the equivalent resistance is:

$$
R_{eq} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3 + \dots + R_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} R_i
$$

---

### Characteristics of Series Circuits

| Characteristic       | Description                                                                  |
| :------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Current**          | The same through every component.                                            |
| **Voltage**          | Divides across each component; sum of individual voltages equals total voltage. |
| **Equivalent Resistance** | Sum of individual resistances. Always greater than any individual resistance. |
| **Circuit Break**    | If one component fails, the entire circuit breaks.                           |

---

### Important Notes

*   Adding more resistors in series *increases* the total resistance of the circuit.
*   This increased resistance leads to a *decrease* in the total current (for a constant voltage source).
*   Series circuits are often used for voltage division or when a specific total resistance is required.

---

### Summary

In a series circuit, current remains constant through all components, while voltage divides. The equivalent resistance is the direct sum of individual resistances, leading to a higher total resistance and generally lower total current.
