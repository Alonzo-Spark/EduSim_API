┌───────────────────────────┐  
│ **Derivation of** $ s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2 $ │  
└───────────────────────────┘  

This equation describes the **displacement** ($ s $) of an object undergoing **uniform acceleration**. Let's derive it step by step using a velocity-time graph approach.

---

### ▪️ **Introduction**

When an object moves with constant acceleration, its velocity changes uniformly with time. We can use the area under a **velocity-time graph** to find the displacement.

This derivation is based on **kinematics** and graphical analysis.

---

### ▪️ **Key Concepts**

- $ s $: displacement  
- $ u $: initial velocity  
- $ v $: final velocity  
- $ a $: acceleration  
- $ t $: time  

We assume **constant (uniform) acceleration**.

---

### ▪️ **Graphical Setup**

Consider a **velocity-time graph** for an object starting with initial velocity $ u $ and accelerating uniformly to final velocity $ v $ in time $ t $.

The graph is a **straight line** with positive slope.

```
Velocity (v)
│     /--------
│    /
│   / 
│  / 
│ / 
│/________________> Time (t)
O     t
```

The **area under this graph** gives the **displacement** $ s $.

---

### ▪️ **Break the Area**

The area under the graph forms a **trapezoid**, which can be split into:
1. A **rectangle** (representing motion at constant initial velocity)
2. A **triangle** (representing the increase in velocity due to acceleration)

- Rectangle: height = $ u $, width = $ t $ → Area = $ u \cdot t $
- Triangle: base = $ t $, height = $ v - u $

From acceleration definition:
$$
a = \frac{v - u}{t} \quad \Rightarrow \quad v - u = at
$$

So, height of triangle = $ at $

Area of triangle:
$$
\frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = \frac{1}{2} \times t \times at = \frac{1}{2}at^2
$$

---

### ▪️ **Add the Areas**

Total displacement = area of rectangle + area of triangle

$$
s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2
$$

### ▪️ **Explain Formula**

- $ ut $: displacement due to initial velocity over time $ t $  
- $ \frac{1}{2}at^2 $: additional displacement due to acceleration  
- The $ \frac{1}{2} $ appears because velocity increases **linearly**, so average velocity during acceleration is $ \frac{u + v}{2} $

---

### ▪️ **Important Notes**

- This equation is valid **only for constant acceleration**
- If $ u = 0 $, the equation simplifies to $ s = \frac{1}{2}at^2 $
- Applies to motion in a straight line (1D)

---

### ▪️ **Applications**

- Free-fall under gravity (e.g., dropping a ball)
- Car accelerating uniformly from initial speed
- Projectile motion (horizontal or vertical components)

---

### ▪️ **Summary**

We derived $ s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2 $ using the **area under a velocity-time graph**, combining:
- Uniform motion ($ ut $)
- Accelerated motion ($ \frac{1}{2}at^2 $)

This is the **second equation of motion**.
