While the electric field describes the <strong>force</strong> a charge experiences, the electric potential describes the <strong>energy</strong> a charge possesses due to its position in the field.

    <!-- Card: Definition - Electric Field -->

        <div class="card-header">
            <span class="card-icon">⚡</span>
            <h2>Definition: Electric Field</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="card-body">
            <p>
                The <strong>electric field</strong> is a region around a charged particle or object within which a force would be exerted on other charged particles or objects. 
                <br><br>
                It is a <span class="tag tag-vector">Vector Quantity</span>, meaning it has both magnitude and direction. By convention, the direction of the electric field is the direction of the force that would be exerted on a <strong>positive</strong> test charge placed in the field.
            </p>
        </div>
    </div>

        </div>
        <div class="card-body">
            <div class="formula-display">
                $$ \vec{E} = \frac{\vec{F}}{q} $$
            </div>
            
            <div class="variable-legend">

        </div>
        <div class="card-body">
            <p>
                <strong>Electric potential</strong> (or simply potential) is the amount of work energy needed to move a unit of electric charge from a reference point to a specific point in an electric field. 
                <br><br>
                It is a <span class="tag tag-scalar">Scalar Quantity</span>, meaning it has magnitude but no direction. It is often compared to height in a gravitational field: just as an object has potential energy due to its height, a charge has potential energy due to its electric potential.
            </p>
        </div>
    </div>
