We can explore the fascinating orbital behavior of a planet when its tangential velocity is reduced below the stable circular speed.

***

### Quick Definition

A **stable circular orbit** occurs when a celestial body's tangential velocity is perfectly balanced by the gravitational pull of the central body, resulting in a constant distance and speed.

***

### Key Concepts

*   **Gravitational Force ($F_g$)**: The attractive force between two objects with mass.
*   **Centripetal Force ($F_c$)**: The force required to keep an object moving in a circular path. In a stable orbit, gravitational force provides the necessary centripetal force.
*   **Tangential Velocity ($v_t$)**: The velocity component perpendicular to the radius vector from the central body.

***

### The Scenario: Reduced Tangential Velocity

Imagine a planet in a stable circular orbit around a star. If its tangential velocity is suddenly reduced below the speed required for a stable circular orbit, the balance between gravitational force and the planet's inertia is disrupted.

***

### Orbital Behavior

1.  **Initial Imbalance**: When the tangential velocity ($v_t$) decreases, the centripetal force required to maintain the original circular path also decreases ($F_c = \frac{mv_t^2}{r}$). However, the gravitational force ($F_g = \frac{GMm}{r^2}$) remains momentarily the same (assuming the distance $r$ hasn't changed yet).
2.  **Gravitational Dominance**: Since the gravitational force is now *greater* than the (reduced) centripetal force, the star's gravity begins to pull the planet *inward* towards itself.
3.  **Descent and Acceleration**: As the planet moves closer to the star, two things happen:
    *   The gravitational force *increases* (due to the inverse square law: $F_g \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$).
    *   The planet's speed *increases* due to the conversion of gravitational potential energy into kinetic energy.
4.  **Elliptical Orbit Formation**: The planet will continue to fall inward, gaining speed, until it reaches its closest point to the star (periapsis). At this point, its velocity will be at its maximum, and it will have enough tangential speed to swing around the star and begin moving *outward* again.
5.  **Ascent and Deceleration**: As the planet moves away from the star, it trades kinetic energy for gravitational potential energy, slowing down as it reaches its farthest point (apoapsis).
6.  **New Stable Orbit**: If the reduction in tangential velocity wasn't so drastic as to cause the planet to collide with the star or completely escape, it will settle into a new, more elliptical orbit with a smaller semi-major axis (meaning its average distance from the star is less than its original circular orbit). The original position where the velocity was reduced will become the apoapsis of this new elliptical orbit.

***

### Main Formula

The **orbital velocity ($v$)** for a circular orbit is given by:

$$ v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}} $$

Where:
*   $G$ is the gravitational constant ($6.674 \times 10^{-11} \text{ N m}^2/\text{kg}^2$)
*   $M$ is the mass of the central body (e.g., star)
*   $r$ is the orbital radius

When the actual tangential velocity ($v_{actual}$) drops below this $v$, the inward pull of gravity becomes dominant.

---
### Explain Formula
This formula calculates the precise speed an object needs to maintain a perfectly circular orbit at a given distance $r$ from a central body of mass $M$. If the object's speed is less than this value, it will fall inward. If it's greater, it will move outward.

### Analogy

Imagine swinging a ball on a string in a perfect circle. The tension in the string represents gravity, and your hand provides the centripetal force. If you suddenly reduce the speed at which you're swinging the ball, the tension (gravity) will pull the ball closer to your hand, causing it to spiral inward before potentially settling into a smaller, elongated path.

***

### Important Notes

*   **Conservation of Energy**: Throughout this process, the total mechanical energy (kinetic + potential) of the planet remains constant (assuming no external forces like atmospheric drag). Energy is simply converted between kinetic and potential forms.
*   **Conservation of Angular Momentum**: The angular momentum of the planet about the star also remains constant. This explains why the planet speeds up as it gets closer to the star (smaller $r$, so $v$ must increase to keep $mvr$ constant).

***

### Summary

Reducing a planet's tangential velocity below the stable circular speed causes the gravitational force to overpower the necessary centripetal force. This leads the planet to fall inward towards the central star, accelerating as it does so. It will then swing around the star and settle into a new, more elliptical orbit with a smaller average distance from the star. The point where the velocity was reduced becomes the apoapsis (farthest point) of this new elliptical path.
