This section explores the relationship between gravity preset scaling and the range of a block sliding down an inclined plane. We will examine the forces involved and how variations in gravitational acceleration affect the block's motion and final horizontal distance.

---

### Key Concepts

Gravity preset scaling refers to a multiplier applied to the standard acceleration due to gravity ($g$). An inclined plane is a simple machine consisting of a sloping surface, and a sliding block is an object moving along this surface, subject to various forces.

---

### Forces on the Inclined Plane

When a block rests on an inclined plane, several forces act upon it:

*   **Gravitational Force ($F_g$)**: Acts vertically downwards. Its magnitude is $mg$, where $m$ is the mass of the block and $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity.
*   **Normal Force ($F_N$)**: Acts perpendicular to the surface of the inclined plane, preventing the block from penetrating the surface.
*   **Frictional Force ($F_f$)**: Acts parallel to the surface, opposing the motion of the block. Its magnitude is $\mu_k F_N$ for kinetic friction, where $\mu_k$ is the coefficient of kinetic friction.

We can decompose the gravitational force into two components:

*   **Component parallel to the plane ($F_{g,x}$)**: $mg \sin(\theta)$
*   **Component perpendicular to the plane ($F_{g,y}$)**: $mg \cos(\theta)$

Where $\theta$ is the angle of inclination of the plane.

---

### Main Formula

The acceleration of the block down the inclined plane ($a$) can be derived using Newton's second law.

The net force acting parallel to the plane is:
$F_{net,x} = F_{g,x} - F_f$
$F_{net,x} = mg \sin(\theta) - \mu_k F_N$

Since the block is in equilibrium perpendicular to the plane:
$F_N = F_{g,y} = mg \cos(\theta)$

Substituting $F_N$ into the net force equation:
$F_{net,x} = mg \sin(\theta) - \mu_k (mg \cos(\theta))$
$F_{net,x} = mg (\sin(\theta) - \mu_k \cos(\theta))$

Using Newton's second law ($F_{net,x} = ma$):
$ma = mg (\sin(\theta) - \mu_k \cos(\theta))$

Therefore, the acceleration of the block down the inclined plane is:

$$
a = g (\sin(\theta) - \mu_k \cos(\theta))
$$

---

### Calculating the Range

To calculate the range, we need to consider two phases of motion:

1.  **Motion down the inclined plane**:
    *   Initial velocity ($v_0$) = 0
    *   Acceleration ($a$) = $g (\sin(\theta) - \mu_k \cos(\theta))$
    *   Distance traveled along the plane ($d$)
    *   Final velocity at the bottom of the plane ($v_f$) can be found using $v_f^2 = v_0^2 + 2ad$.
        $v_f = \sqrt{2ad}$

2.  **Projectile motion after leaving the plane**:
    Assuming the block leaves the plane horizontally at height $h$ and with velocity $v_f$.
    *   **Time of flight ($t$)**: The time it takes for the block to fall from height $h$ to the ground.
        $h = \frac{1}{2}gt^2 \implies t = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}$
    *   **Horizontal range ($R$)**: The horizontal distance covered during the time of flight.
        $R = v_f t$

Combining these, if the plane has length $L$ and angle $\theta$, then the height $h = L \sin(\theta)$.

$$
R = \sqrt{2aL} \sqrt{\frac{2L \sin(\theta)}{g}}
$$

Substituting the expression for $a$:

$$
R = \sqrt{2 g (\sin(\theta) - \mu_k \cos(\theta)) L} \sqrt{\frac{2L \sin(\theta)}{g}}
$$

$$
R = \sqrt{4 L^2 (\sin(\theta) - \mu_k \cos(\theta)) \sin(\theta)}
$$

$$
R = 2L \sqrt{(\sin(\theta) - \mu_k \cos(\theta)) \sin(\theta)}
$$

Notice that the gravitational acceleration $g$ cancels out in the final range formula, *assuming the block leaves the ramp horizontally*.

However, if the block *slides off the end of the ramp and then acts as a projectile*, the horizontal velocity component at the end of the ramp is $v_f \cos(\theta)$ and the vertical velocity component is $v_f \sin(\theta)$. The height from which it falls is $L \sin(\theta)$.

Let's re-evaluate the projectile motion more carefully.

At the end of the ramp:
*   Velocity magnitude: $v_f = \sqrt{2aL}$
*   Horizontal component of velocity: $v_x = v_f \cos(\theta)$
*   Vertical component of velocity: $v_y = -v_f \sin(\theta)$ (downwards if $\theta$ is measured from horizontal upwards)
*   Initial height: $y_0 = L \sin(\theta)$

Projectile motion equations:
$x(t) = v_x t$
$y(t) = y_0 + v_y t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$

We want to find $t$ when $y(t) = 0$:
$0 = L \sin(\theta) - v_f \sin(\theta) t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$

This is a quadratic equation for $t$.

$t = \frac{v_f \sin(\theta) \pm \sqrt{v_f^2 \sin^2(\theta) + 2gL \sin(\theta)}}{g}$

Since $t$ must be positive, we take the positive root.

$t = \frac{v_f \sin(\theta) + \sqrt{v_f^2 \sin^2(\theta) + 2gL \sin(\theta)}}{g}$

Then the range $R = v_x t = v_f \cos(\theta) t$.

Substituting $v_f = \sqrt{2g(\sin(\theta) - \mu_k \cos(\theta))L}$:

$$
R = \sqrt{2g(\sin(\theta) - \mu_k \cos(\theta))L} \cos(\theta) \left( \frac{\sqrt{2g(\sin(\theta) - \mu_k \cos(\theta))L} \sin(\theta) + \sqrt{2g(\sin(\theta) - \mu_k \cos(\theta))L \sin^2(\theta) + 2gL \sin(\theta)}}{g} \right)
$$

This expression is quite complex. Let's simplify by noting the dependence on $g$.
Let $a_{eff} = g(\sin(\theta) - \mu_k \cos(\theta))$.
So, $v_f = \sqrt{2a_{eff}L}$.

$t = \frac{\sqrt{2a_{eff}L} \sin(\theta) + \sqrt{2a_{eff}L \sin^2(\theta) + 2gL \sin(\theta)}}{g}$

$R = \sqrt{2a_{eff}L} \cos(\theta) \left( \frac{\sqrt{2a_{eff}L} \sin(\theta) + \sqrt{2a_{eff}L \sin^2(\theta) + 2gL \sin(\theta)}}{g} \right)$

We can see that $g$ appears in the denominator and inside the square roots.
Let $g_{scaled}$ be the new gravitational acceleration.
Then $a_{eff, scaled} = g_{scaled}(\sin(\theta) - \mu_k \cos(\theta))$.
$v_{f, scaled} = \sqrt{2a_{eff, scaled}L} = \sqrt{2g_{scaled}(\sin(\theta) - \mu_k \cos(\theta))L}$.

$t_{scaled} = \frac{\sqrt{2g_{scaled}(\sin(\theta) - \mu_k \cos(\theta))L} \sin(\theta) + \sqrt{2g_{scaled}(\sin(\theta) - \mu_k \cos(\theta))L \sin^2(\theta) + 2g_{scaled}L \sin(\theta)}}{g_{scaled}}$

$R_{scaled} = v_{f, scaled} \cos(\theta) t_{scaled}$

It is clear that $g_{scaled}$ does not simply cancel out.
If $g_{scaled}$ increases, $v_{f, scaled}$ increases. This would tend to increase $R$.
However, $g_{scaled}$ also appears in the denominator of $t_{scaled}$, which would tend to decrease $t$.
The overall effect is not immediately obvious without further simplification or numerical analysis.

Let's consider the case where $\mu_k = 0$ (no friction).
Then $a = g \sin(\theta)$.
$v_f = \sqrt{2g \sin(\theta) L}$.

$t = \frac{\sqrt{2g \sin(\theta) L} \sin(\theta) + \sqrt{2g \sin(\theta) L \sin^2(\theta) + 2gL \sin(\theta)}}{g}$
$t = \frac{\sqrt{2gL \sin(\theta)} \sin(\theta) + \sqrt{2gL \sin(\theta)(\sin^2(\theta) + 1)}}{g}$

$R = \sqrt{2g \sin(\theta) L} \cos(\theta) \left( \frac{\sqrt{2gL \sin(\theta)} \sin(\theta) + \sqrt{2gL \sin(\theta)(\sin^2(\theta) + 1)}}{g} \right)$

$R = \frac{2gL \sin(\theta) \cos(\theta) \sin(\theta) + \sqrt{2gL \sin(\theta)} \cos(\theta) \sqrt{2gL \sin(\theta)(\sin^2(\theta)+1)}}{g}$

This is still quite messy. Let's look at the scaling aspect.

Let $g' = k \cdot g$, where $k$ is the gravity preset scaling factor.

$v_f' = \sqrt{2(k g)(\sin(\theta) - \mu_k \cos(\theta))L} = \sqrt{k} v_f$ (where $v_f$ is original final velocity)

$t' = \frac{\sqrt{k} v_f \sin(\theta) + \sqrt{k v_f^2 \sin^2(\theta) + 2(k g)L \sin(\theta)}}{k g}$
$t' = \frac{\sqrt{k} v_f \sin(\theta) + \sqrt{k (v_f^2 \sin^2(\theta) + 2gL \sin(\theta))}}{k g}$
$t' = \frac{\sqrt{k} (v_f \sin(\theta) + \sqrt{v_f^2 \sin^2(\theta) + 2gL \sin(\theta)})}{k g}$
$t' = \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} t$ (where $t$ is original time of flight)

So, if gravity scales by $k$, $v_f$ scales by $\sqrt{k}$ and $t$ scales by $1/\sqrt{k}$.

Then $R' = v_x' t' = (v_f' \cos(\theta)) t' = (\sqrt{k} v_f \cos(\theta)) (\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} t) = v_f \cos(\theta) t = R$.

This is a very important result! **The range of the block is independent of the gravitational acceleration scaling factor.**

Let's verify this.
Initial acceleration $a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k \cos\theta)$.
Final velocity $v_f = \sqrt{2aL} = \sqrt{2g(\sin\theta - \mu_k \cos\theta)L}$.
Projectile motion:
Initial horizontal velocity $v_{ix} = v_f \cos\theta$.
Initial vertical velocity $v_{iy} = -v_f \sin\theta$.
Initial height $y_0 = L \sin\theta$.

Time of flight $t$ satisfies: $y(t) = y_0 + v_{iy}t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2 = 0$.
$L \sin\theta - (v_f \sin\theta)t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2 = 0$.
$t = \frac{-(-v_f \sin\theta) \pm \sqrt{(-v_f \sin\theta)^2 - 4(-\frac{1}{2}g)(L \sin\theta)}}{2(-\frac{1}{2}g)}$
This is incorrect. The quadratic formula is for $Ax^2+Bx+C=0$.
Here, $-\frac{1}{2}gt^2 - (v_f \sin\theta)t + L \sin\theta = 0$.
$t = \frac{v_f \sin\theta \pm \sqrt{(v_f \sin\theta)^2 - 4(-\frac{1}{2}g)(L \sin\theta)}}{2(-\frac{1}{2}g)}$
$t = \frac{v_f \sin\theta \pm \sqrt{v_f^2 \sin^2\theta + 2gL \sin\theta}}{-g}$
Since $t$ must be positive, we need the negative root:
$t = \frac{-(v_f \sin\theta) + \sqrt{v_f^2 \sin^2\theta + 2gL \sin\theta}}{g}$

Now, substitute $v_f = \sqrt{2g(\sin\theta - \mu_k \cos\theta)L}$:
$v_f^2 = 2g(\sin\theta - \mu_k \cos\theta)L$.

$t = \frac{-\sqrt{2g(\sin\theta - \mu_k \cos\theta)L} \sin\theta + \sqrt{2g(\sin\theta - \mu_k \cos\theta)L \sin^2\theta + 2gL \sin\theta}}{g}$

Let's factor out $g$ from the numerator terms.
Numerator terms under square root:
$\sqrt{2gL (\sin\theta - \mu_k \cos\theta) \sin^2\theta}$
$\sqrt{2gL \sin\theta}$

This is where the $g$ dependence comes in. The terms under the square root are not simply $g \times (\text{something})$.
Let $A = 2L(\sin\theta - \mu_k \cos\theta)$.
Let $B = 2L \sin\theta$.

$t = \frac{-\sqrt{gA}\sin\theta + \sqrt{gA\sin^2\theta + gB}}{g}$
$t = \frac{-\sqrt{g}\sqrt{A}\sin\theta + \sqrt{g}\sqrt{A\sin^2\theta + B}}{g}$
$t = \frac{\sqrt{g}(-\sqrt{A}\sin\theta + \sqrt{A\sin^2\theta + B})}{g}$
$t = \frac{-\sqrt{A}\sin\theta + \sqrt{A\sin^2\theta + B}}{\sqrt{g}}$

So, $t \propto 1/\sqrt{g}$. This confirms our previous deduction.

Now for the range $R$:
$R = v_{ix} t = (v_f \cos\theta) t$
$v_f = \sqrt{gA}$ (where $A$ is defined above).
$R = (\sqrt{gA} \cos\theta) \left( \frac{-\sqrt{A}\sin\theta + \sqrt{A\sin^2\theta + B}}{\sqrt{g}} \right)$
$R = \cos\theta \left( -\sqrt{A^2}\sin\theta + \sqrt{A(A\sin^2\theta + B)} \right)$
$R = \cos\theta \left( -A\sin\theta + \sqrt{A^2\sin^2\theta + AB} \right)$

This final expression for $R$ **does not contain $g$**.

Therefore, the gravity preset scaling **does not influence the range** of the inclined plane sliding block.

| **Phase 2: Projectile Motion**           | After leaving the ramp, the block becomes a projectile. Its trajectory is governed by its velocity components at the point of departure and the acceleration due to gravity. The key finding is that the horizontal range of the block is independent of the gravitational acceleration. This is because both the acceleration down the plane and the time of flight for projectile motion are scaled inversely with $\sqrt{g}$, effectively canceling out the $g$ dependence in the final range calculation. |

---

### Conclusion

Surprisingly, the gravity preset scaling **does not influence the final horizontal range** of a block sliding down an inclined plane and then becoming a projectile. While a higher gravitational acceleration would make the block slide down the ramp faster, it would also cause it to fall faster once it leaves the ramp, and these two effects cancel each other out in the calculation of the horizontal range.
