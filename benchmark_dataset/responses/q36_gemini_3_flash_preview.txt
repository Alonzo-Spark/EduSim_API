:::section{title="Introduction"}
Oscillations and waves form the basis of many physical phenomena, from the ticking of a grandfather clock to the transmission of radio signals. At the heart of these periodic movements lies **Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)**, a specific type of periodic motion where the restoring force is directly proportional to the displacement.
:::

:::section{title="Quick Definition"}
**Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)** is defined as a periodic motion in which the acceleration of the object is directly proportional to its displacement from a fixed equilibrium position and is always directed toward that position.
:::

:::section{title="Key Concepts"}
*   **Equilibrium Position:** The central point where no net force acts on the object ($x = 0$).
*   **Restoring Force:** A force that always acts to push or pull the object back toward the equilibrium position.
*   **Displacement ($x$):** The distance and direction of the object from its equilibrium position.
*   **Amplitude ($A$):** The maximum displacement from the equilibrium position.
:::

:::formula-card{title="The Governing Law (Hooke's Law Form)"}
$$
F = -kx
$$
**Where:**
*   $F$ is the Restoring Force (N)
*   $k$ is the Force Constant (N/m)
*   $x$ is the Displacement (m)
*   The **negative sign** indicates the force is always opposite to the direction of displacement.

:::

:::section{title="Characteristics of SHM"}
To qualify as Simple Harmonic Motion, the system must exhibit these three specific traits:
1.  **Periodic Nature:** The motion repeats itself in equal intervals of time (Period, $T$).
2.  **Proportionality:** The magnitude of acceleration/force is proportional to the distance from the center.
3.  **Directionality:** The acceleration is always directed toward the equilibrium point (opposite to displacement).
:::

:::formula-card{title="The Equation of Acceleration"}
$$
a = -\omega^2 x
$$
**Where:**
*   $a$ is Acceleration ($m/s^2$)
*   $\omega$ is Angular Frequency ($rad/s$)
*   $x$ is Displacement ($m$)

:::section{title="Comparison: SHM vs. Periodic Motion"}
| Feature | Periodic Motion | Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Repetition** | Repeats at fixed intervals | Repeats at fixed intervals |
| **Path** | Can be any closed path (e.g., Earth's orbit) | Must be back-and-forth (oscillatory) |
| **Force Requirement** | Net force varies | Force must be proportional to $-x$ |
| **Example** | Uniform Circular Motion | A vibrating tuning fork |
:::

:::section{title="Important Notes"}
> [!IMPORTANT]
> Not all oscillations are SHM. For example, a bouncing ball is periodic, but because the force (gravity) is constant and doesn't change with displacement, it is **not** performing Simple Harmonic Motion.
:::

:::section{title="Suggested Questions"}
1.  **Conceptual:** Why is the acceleration in SHM maximum at the amplitude and zero at the equilibrium position?
2.  **Mathematical:** If the displacement of a particle is given by $x = A \sin(\omega t)$, derive the expression for its velocity.
3.  **Application:** Describe how a simple pendulum approximates SHM only at small angles.
:::
