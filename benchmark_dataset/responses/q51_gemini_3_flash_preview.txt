:::section{title="Introduction"}
In classical mechanics, a **Simple Pendulum** consists of a point mass (bob) suspended by a massless, unstretchable string. A common misconception is that a heavier "bob" will swing faster or slower. Today, we analyze the relationship between **Mass ($m$)** and the **Period ($T$)**.
:::

:::section{title="Quick Definition"}
**The Period ($T$)** is the total time taken for the pendulum to complete one full back-and-forth cycle (oscillation).
:::

:::section{title="Formula Card"}
### The Period of a Simple Pendulum
$$
T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}
$$

**Variable Breakdown:**
*   $T$: Period (seconds)
*   $L$: Length of the string (meters)
*   $g$: Acceleration due to gravity ($\approx 9.81 \, m/s^2$)
*   $m$: Mass of the bob (kilograms) — *Note its absence in the formula.*

:::

:::section{title="Key Concepts: The Role of Mass"}
Contrary to intuition, **changing the mass does not affect the period** of a simple pendulum (assuming air resistance is negligible and the angle is small).

*   **Gravitational Force:** A larger mass experiences a stronger gravitational pull ($F = mg$).
*   **Inertia:** A larger mass also has greater resistance to changes in motion ($F = ma$).
*   **The Cancellation:** These two effects cancel each other out perfectly. The acceleration of the bob is independent of its mass.
:::

:::section{title="Data Comparison Table"}
| Mass ($kg$) | Length ($m$) | Gravity ($m/s^2$) | Calculated Period ($s$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 0.5 | 1.0 | 9.81 | ~2.01 |
| 1.0 | 1.0 | 9.81 | ~2.01 |
| 5.0 | 1.0 | 9.81 | ~2.01 |
| 10.0 | 1.0 | 9.81 | ~2.01 |

**Observation:** As mass increases by a factor of 20, the period remains constant at $\approx 2.01$ seconds.
:::

:::section{title="Important Notes"}
*   **Small Angle Approximation:** This independence holds true primarily for small angles (usually $< 15^\circ$).
*   **Air Resistance:** In a real-world "Live Sandbox," a very light object with a large surface area (like a balloon) will be slowed by air resistance, making it *appear* that mass matters.
*   **Center of Mass:** If changing the mass changes the *effective length* (e.g., using a very large physical sphere), the period will change because $L$ changed, not because of the mass itself.
:::
