To derive the expression for the equivalent resistance in a parallel combination of resistors, we'll analyze the circuit behavior step by step. Here's the clear, structured derivation:

---

### **Step-by-Step Derivation**

#### **1. Circuit Setup**
- Consider **three resistors** \(R_1\), \(R_2\), and \(R_3\) connected in parallel between points **A** and **B**.
- A **voltage source** \(V\) is applied across A and B.
- The potential difference (voltage) across each resistor is **identical** and equals \(V\) (parallel circuit property).

#### **2. Current Flow**
- Let \(I\) be the **total current** from the voltage source.
- This current splits into branch currents \(I_1\), \(I_2\), and \(I_3\) through \(R_1\), \(R_2\), and \(R_3\), respectively.
- By Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL):  
  \[
  I = I_1 + I_2 + I_3 \quad (1)
  \]

#### **3. Apply Ohm's Law**
- For each resistor:  
  \[
  I_1 = \frac{V}{R_1}, \quad I_2 = \frac{V}{R_2}, \quad I_3 = \frac{V}{R_3} \quad (2)
  \]

#### **4. Substitute into KCL Equation**
- Substitute (2) into (1):  
  \[
  I = \frac{V}{R_1} + \frac{V}{R_2} + \frac{V}{R_3} \quad (3)
  \]

#### **5. Define Equivalent Resistance**
- Let \(R_{\text{eq}}\) be the single resistance that replaces the parallel combination. By Ohm’s Law:  
  \[
  I = \frac{V}{R_{\text{eq}}} \quad (4)
  \]

#### **6. Combine Equations (3) and (4)**
- Equate (3) and (4):  
  \[
  \frac{V}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{V}{R_1} + \frac{V}{R_2} + \frac{V}{R_3}
  \]
- Divide both sides by \(V\):  
  \[
  \frac{1}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3} \quad (5)
  \]

#### **7. Generalize for \(n\) Resistors**
- For \(n\) resistors in parallel:  
  \[
  \boxed{\dfrac{1}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2} + \cdots + \dfrac{1}{R_n}}
  \]

---

### **Key Properties**
1. **Voltage Uniformity**:  
   Voltage across all parallel resistors is the same.
2. **Current Division**:  
   Current through each resistor is inversely proportional to its resistance.
3. **Equivalent Resistance**:  
   \(R_{\text{eq}}\) is **always less than** the smallest resistor in the combination.

---

### **Formula Card**
```math
\boxed{R_{\text{eq}} = \left( \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2} + \cdots + \dfrac{1}{R_n} \right)^{-1}}
```

| **Symbol** | **Meaning**                          |
|------------|--------------------------------------|
| \(R_{\text{eq}}\) | Equivalent resistance of the parallel combination |
| \(R_1, R_2, \dots, R_n\) | Individual resistances in parallel   |

---

### **Why This Matters**
- Parallel configurations **reduce total resistance**, allowing higher current flow than any single branch.
- Applications include household wiring, electronic circuits, and safety devices (e.g., fuses).

---

### **Verify with a Case**
**Example**: Calculate \(R_{\text{eq}}\) for \(R_1 = 2\ \Omega\), \(R_2 = 3\ \Omega\), and \(R_3 = 6\ \Omega\) in parallel.  
**Solution**:  
\[
\frac{1}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{3}{6} + \frac{2}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{6}{6} = 1 \implies R_{\text{eq}} = 1\ \Omega
\]  
*Note*: \(1\ \Omega < 2\ \Omega\) (smallest resistor), as expected.

---

### **Summary**
- **Reciprocal rule**: The reciprocal of \(R_{\text{eq}}\) is the sum of reciprocals of individual resistances.
- **Practical insight**: Adding resistors in parallel **lowers total resistance** and **increases total current**.
