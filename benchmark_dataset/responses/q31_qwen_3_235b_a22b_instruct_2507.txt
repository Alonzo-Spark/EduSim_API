<div class="section-card introduction">
  <h2>Introduction</h2>
  <p>When heat is transferred to a substance, its temperature usually changes. The extent of this change depends on the material's inherent ability to store thermal energy. Two key thermal properties—<strong>heat capacity</strong> and <strong>specific heat capacity</strong>—quantify this behavior. These concepts are essential for understanding how different materials respond to heat input.</p>
</div>

<div class="section-card definition">
  <h2>Definition</h2>
  <p><strong>Heat Capacity</strong> is the amount of heat energy required to raise the temperature of an entire object or substance by one degree Celsius (or one Kelvin).</p>
  <p><strong>Specific Heat Capacity</strong> is the amount of heat energy required to raise the temperature of <em>one kilogram</em> of a substance by one degree Celsius (or one Kelvin).</p>
</div>

<div class="section-card formula-card">
  <h2>Main Formula</h2>
  <div class="formula-display">
    $$
    Q = m c \Delta T
    $$
  </div>

</div>

<div class="section-card key-concepts">
  <h2>Key Concepts</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Q</strong>: Heat energy transferred (in joules, J)</li>
    <li><strong>m</strong>: Mass of the substance (in kilograms, kg)</li>
    <li><strong>c</strong>: Specific heat capacity (in J/kg·K or J/kg·°C)</li>
    <li><strong>ΔT</strong>: Change in temperature (in K or °C)</li>
  </ul>
</div>

<div class="section-card applications">
  <h2>Applications</h2>
  <ul>
    <li>Designing cooling systems (e.g., car radiators use water due to its high specific heat capacity).</li>
    <li>Climate science—oceans moderate temperature changes due to high heat capacity.</li>
    <li>Cooking—materials like aluminum and copper are used in cookware for their thermal properties.</li>
    <li>Thermal storage systems in solar energy applications.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

<div class="section-card advantages">
  <h2>Advantages of High Specific Heat Capacity</h2>
  <ul>
    <li>Stabilizes temperature (e.g., water buffers temperature changes in living organisms).</li>
    <li>Efficient for heat storage and transport (e.g., water in heating systems).</li>
  </ul>
</div>

<div class="section-card disadvantages">
  <h2>Disadvantages of High Specific Heat Capacity</h2>
  <ul>
    <li>Requires more energy to heat up (e.g., heating water consumes more energy than heating metals).</li>
    <li>Slower response in temperature-sensitive applications.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

<div class="section-card important-notes">
  <h2>Important Notes</h2>
  <ul>
    <li>Temperature change in °C is equivalent to change in K.</li>
    <li>Specific heat capacity is an intrinsic property—same for all samples of a pure substance.</li>
    <li>Heat capacity is an extensive property—depends on the amount of material.</li>
    <li>The formula $ Q = m c \Delta T $ assumes no phase change occurs.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

<div class="section-card summary">
  <h2>Summary</h2>
  <p><strong>Heat capacity</strong> measures how much heat an object can absorb for a given temperature rise and depends on both mass and material. <strong>Specific heat capacity</strong> is a normalized version—per unit mass—making it a material-specific constant. Together, they are fundamental in thermodynamics, engineering, and environmental science.</p>
</div>
