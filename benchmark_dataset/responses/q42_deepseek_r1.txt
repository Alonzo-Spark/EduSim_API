### Electric Field and Electric Potential

#### Quick Definitions  
┌───────────────────────────────────┐  
│ **Electric Field (E)**            │  
│ Region around a charged particle  │  
│ where forces act on other charges │  
│ *Units:* N/C or V/m               │  
└───────────────────────────────────┘  

┌───────────────────────────────────┐  
│ **Electric Potential (V)**        │  
│ Potential energy per unit charge  │  
│ at a point in an electric field   │  
│ *Units:* Volts (V)                │  
└───────────────────────────────────┘  

---

#### Key Concepts  
**1. Electric Field**  
- **Definition:** Vector field representing force per unit charge  
- **Direction:** Points *away* from (+) charges, *toward* (-) charges  
- **Formula for Point Charge:**  
  $$
  \vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q}{r^2} \hat{r}
  $$  
  <details>  
  <summary>**Formula Explanation**</summary>  
  - \( \epsilon_0 \): Permittivity of free space (\(8.85 \times 10^{-12} \, \text{C}^2/\text{N·m}^2\))  
  - \( q \): Source charge  
  - \( r \): Distance from charge  
  - \( \hat{r} \): Unit vector direction  
  </details>  

**2. Electric Potential**  
- **Definition:** Scalar field representing work needed to move a unit positive charge from infinity to a point  
- **Zero Reference:** Defined as \( V = 0 \) at infinity  
- **Formula for Point Charge:**  
  $$
  V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q}{r}
  $$  
  <details>  
  <summary>**Formula Explanation**</summary>  
  - Same constants as electric field  
  - Note the \( \frac{1}{r} \) dependence (vs. \( \frac{1}{r^2} \) for E-field)  
  </details>  

---

#### Relationship Between E and V  
┌───────────────────────────────┐  
│ **Fundamental Connection**    │  
│ \( \vec{E} = -\nabla V \)     │  
│ *Where \( \nabla \) is the*   │  
│ *gradient operator*           │  
└───────────────────────────────┘  

**Key Implications:**  
1. E-field points downhill along the steepest decrease of V  
2. Equipotential surfaces (V = constant) are perpendicular to E-field lines  
3. Potential difference between two points:  
   $$
   \Delta V = -\int_a^b \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}
   $$  

---

#### Comparative Summary  
| Property              | Electric Field (\(\vec{E}\))       | Electric Potential (\(V\))       |  
|-----------------------|------------------------------------|----------------------------------|  
| **Nature**            | Vector (magnitude + direction)     | Scalar (magnitude only)          |  
| **Source**            | Charges                            | Charges *or* changing B-fields   |  
| **Dependence**        | \( \frac{1}{r^2} \) (point charge)| \( \frac{1}{r} \) (point charge) |  
| **Zero Location**     | Can be zero at points ≠ infinity   | Always zero at infinity          |  
| **Measurable Effect** | Force on test charge (\( \vec{F} = q\vec{E} \)) | Work per unit charge             |  

---

#### Practical Applications  
- **Capacitors:** \( E = \frac{V}{d} \) (uniform field between plates)  
- **Electronics:** Potential differences drive current  
- **Bioelectricity:** Nerve signals propagate via voltage gradients  

---

#### Common Misconceptions  
⚠️ **Myth:** "Potential is just another name for field strength."  
✅ **Reality:** Potential is energy-related, while field is force-related.  
- Example: E-field can be zero where V is nonzero (e.g., center of charged ring).

---

**Summary Insight:**  
> The electric field (\(\vec{E}\)) tells charges *how to move*, while the potential (\(V\)) tells them *where to go*. Together, they describe electromagnetism's fundamental forces.
