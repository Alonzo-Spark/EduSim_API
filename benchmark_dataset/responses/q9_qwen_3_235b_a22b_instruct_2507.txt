# 📘 Uniform Circular Motion & Centripetal Acceleration  
*An Interactive Physics Lesson*

---

## ▦ Introduction

When an object moves in a perfect circle at a constant speed, it's undergoing **uniform circular motion**. Though the speed doesn't change, the *direction* does — and that makes all the difference in physics.

Because velocity is a vector (has magnitude and direction), changing direction means the object is **accelerating** — even if it's not speeding up or slowing down.

This acceleration is called **centripetal acceleration**, and it always points toward the center of the circle.

---

## ▦ Quick Definition

**Uniform Circular Motion**  
Motion of an object traveling at constant speed along a circular path.

**Centripetal Acceleration**  
The acceleration directed toward the center of a circular path that keeps an object in uniform circular motion.

> 🔁 It’s *not* a new kind of force — it’s the *result* of forces like tension, gravity, or friction acting to keep something moving in a circle.

---

## ▦ Key Concepts

- The speed is constant, but velocity changes continuously due to changing direction.
- Acceleration is always **perpendicular** to the velocity vector and points **inward**.
- No component of centripetal force acts forward or backward — only inward.
- Objects do **not** experience an outward "centrifugal force" in an inertial frame — that’s a fictitious force seen in rotating frames.

---

## ▦ Main Formula

$$
a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}
$$

│                                    │

└────────────────────────────────────┘  

### Variables:
- $ a_c $: centripetal acceleration (m/s²)  
- $ v $: linear speed (m/s)  
- $ r $: radius of the circular path (m)

💡 The greater the speed, the larger the acceleration needed.  
💡 The tighter the turn (smaller $ r $), the greater the acceleration.

---

## ▦ Alternate Formula (Angular Version)

Since $ v = \omega r $, where $ \omega $ is angular speed (rad/s), we can rewrite:

$$
a_c = \omega^2 r
$$

---

## ▦ Direction of Vectors

| Vector        | Direction                        |
|---------------|----------------------------------|
| Velocity ($ \vec{v} $)   | Tangent to the circle          |
| Acceleration ($ \vec{a_c} $) | Radially inward (→ center)     |
| Net Force ($ \vec{F_c} $)    | Same as $ a_c $: toward center |

📌 Remember:  
$$
\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = m\vec{a_c}
\Rightarrow
F_c = \frac{mv^2}{r}
$$

This **centripetal force** is not a new force — it's the *net* force causing circular motion.

---

## ▦ Real-World Applications

| Scenario                     | Source of Centripetal Force         |
|-----------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Car turning on flat road     | Friction between tires and road      |
| Satellite orbiting Earth     | Gravitational pull                   |
| Ball on string being swung   | Tension in the string                |
| Roller coaster loop          | Normal force from track              |

⚠️ If the required centripetal force isn't available (e.g., ice reduces friction), the object flies off **tangent** to the circle — not radially outward!

---

## ▦ Common Misconceptions

❌ “Centrifugal force pulls the object outward.”  
✅ In an inertial frame, there is **no outward force**. The object tends to go straight due to inertia — the center-directed force pulls it *into* the curved path.

❌ “Fast-moving objects in circles have high tangential acceleration.”  
✅ In *uniform* circular motion, tangential acceleration is **zero** — only centripetal (radial) acceleration exists.

---

## ▦ Important Notes

- Centripetal acceleration increases with the **square of the speed**: double the speed → **four times** the acceleration.
- Halving the radius also **doubles** the acceleration.
- All objects in uniform circular motion are **constantly accelerating**, so they are **not** in equilibrium.

## ▦ Suggested Questions

1. A car rounds a curve of radius 50 m at 20 m/s. What is its centripetal acceleration?  
2. Why does a passenger feel "pushed outward" in a turning car if no real outward force exists?  
3. Derive $ a_c = v^2/r $ using vector analysis of velocity change over time.  
4. How would doubling both speed and radius affect centripetal acceleration?

---

## ▦ Summary

Uniform circular motion involves:
- Constant speed
- Changing velocity (due to direction)
- Center-seeking acceleration: $ a_c = v^2/r $
- A net force toward the center provided by real forces (friction, gravity, tension)

🔁 Motion is repetitive and governed by precise mathematical relationships — foundational for understanding orbits, machinery, and even atomic models.
