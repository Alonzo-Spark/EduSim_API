### **Chapter: Mechanics & Energetics**
# The Work-Energy Theorem

| Section | Content Type |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Topic** | Work-Energy Theorem |

---

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ **Quick Definition**                                                     │
│                                                                          │
│ The **Work-Energy Theorem** states that the net work done by the sum of  │
│ all forces acting on an object is equal to the change in its kinetic     │
│ energy.                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

### **Key Concepts**
*   **Net Work ($W_{net}$):** The total work done by all external forces (conservative and non-conservative).
*   **Kinetic Energy ($K$):** The energy an object possesses due to its motion.
*   **Scalar Nature:** Work and Energy are both scalars, meaning direction is not considered, only magnitude.

---

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ **Main Formula**                                                         │
│                                                                          │
│ $$ W_{net} = \Delta K = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_i^2 $$         │
│                                                                          │
│ **[ Explain Formula ]**                                                  │
│ This equation shows that work is the mechanism by which energy is        │
│ transferred into or out of a system's motion.                            │
│                                                                          │
│ **[ Open Formula Lab ]**                                                 │
│ *   $W_{net}$: Total Work (Joules)                                       │
│ *   $m$: Mass (kg)                                                       │
│ *   $v_f$: Final Velocity (m/s)                                          │
│ *   $v_i$: Initial Velocity (m/s)                                        │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

---

### **Derivation (Constant Force)**

We derive the theorem using Newton’s Second Law and Kinematic equations for a constant force acting in the direction of displacement.

1.  **Start with Newton's Second Law:**
    $$ F = ma $$

2.  **Use the 3rd Equation of Motion (Kinematics):**
    $$ v_f^2 = v_i^2 + 2as $$
    *Rearranging for acceleration ($a$):*
    $$ a = \frac{v_f^2 - v_i^2}{2s} $$

3.  **Substitute acceleration into the Force equation:**
    $$ F = m \left( \frac{v_f^2 - v_i^2}{2s} \right) $$

4.  **Calculate Work ($W = F \cdot s$):**
    $$ W = \left[ m \left( \frac{v_f^2 - v_i^2}{2s} \right) \right] \cdot s $$
    *The displacement ($s$) cancels out.*

5.  **Final Result:**
    $$ W = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_i^2 $$

---

### **Important Notes**
*   **Positive Work:** If $W_{net} > 0$, the kinetic energy increases (the object speeds up).
*   **Negative Work:** If $W_{net} < 0$, the kinetic energy decreases (the object slows down).
*   **Zero Work:** If the force is perpendicular to the displacement (e.g., centripetal force), no work is done and speed remains constant.

### **Suggested Questions**

1.  **Conceptual:** A car doubles its speed. By what factor does its kinetic energy increase, and how much more work is required to stop it?
2.  **Calculation:** A 1500kg car travels at 10 m/s. If a constant braking force of 3000N is applied, calculate the distance it travels before coming to a stop using the Work-Energy Theorem.
3.  **Advanced:** How does the theorem apply when the force is not constant (Variable Force)? *(Hint: Integration of $F \cdot dx$)*.
