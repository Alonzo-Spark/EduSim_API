<Section type="Introduction">
  Understanding motion begins with two fundamental concepts: **distance** and **displacement**. Though often used interchangeably in everyday language, in physics they have distinct meanings. Grasping the difference is crucial for studying kinematics — the branch of physics that describes motion.
</Section>

<Section type="Definition" title="Distance">
  <p><strong>Distance</strong> is the total length of the path traveled by an object, regardless of direction. It is a <em>scalar quantity</em>, meaning it only has magnitude (no direction).</p>
</Section>

<Section type="Definition" title="Displacement">
  <p><strong>Displacement</strong> is the change in position of an object from its starting point to its final point, measured in a straight line. It is a <em>vector quantity</em>, meaning it has both magnitude and direction.</p>
</Section>

<Section type="Key Concepts">
  <ul>
    <li>Distance depends on the <strong>path taken</strong>.</li>
    <li>Displacement depends only on the <strong>initial and final positions</strong>.</li>
    <li>Distance is always <strong>positive or zero</strong>.</li>
    <li>Displacement can be <strong>positive, negative, or zero</strong>, depending on direction.</li>
    <li>Distance ≥ |Displacement| — the distance traveled is always greater than or equal to the magnitude of displacement.</li>
  </ul>
</Section>

<Section type="Examples">
  <h4>Example 1: Walking in a Straight Line</h4>
  <p>A person walks 5 meters east in a straight line.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Distance</strong>: 5 m</li>
    <li><strong>Displacement</strong>: 5 m east</li>
  </ul>
  <p>→ Here, distance and displacement are equal in magnitude and direction since the path is straight and unidirectional.</p>

  <h4>Example 2: Round Trip</h4>
  <p>You walk 3 km to a park and then return home.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Distance</strong>: 3 km + 3 km = 6 km</li>
    <li><strong>Displacement</strong>: 0 km (you ended where you started)</li>
  </ul>
  <p>→ Distance accounts for the full journey; displacement is zero because there's no net change in position.</p>

  <h4>Example 3: Moving in Two Dimensions</h4>
  <p>A person walks 3 m east, then 4 m north.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Distance</strong>: 3 m + 4 m = 7 m</li>
    <li><strong>Displacement</strong>: Straight-line distance from start to end.</li>
  </ul>

  <FormulaCard
    formula="\text{Displacement} = \sqrt{(3)^2 + (4)^2} = 5\ \text{m}"
    title="Magnitude of Displacement"
    description="Using the Pythagorean theorem for perpendicular motion."
    variables={{
      "3\ \\text{m}": "eastward movement",
      "4\ \\text{m}": "northward movement",
      "5\ \\text{m}": "resultant displacement"
    }}
  />

  <p>The displacement is 5 m in a direction 53.1° north of east.</p>
</Section>

<Section type="Applications">
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Navigation systems</strong> use displacement to calculate the shortest route (as the crow flies).</li>
    <li><strong>Fitness trackers</strong> report distance traveled, not displacement.</li>
    <li><strong>Physics simulations</strong> rely on displacement to determine velocity and acceleration.</li>
  </ul>
</Section>

<Section type="Advantages">
  <h4>Why Use Distance?</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Useful for measuring fuel consumption (depends on total path length).</li>
    <li>Important in sports (e.g., marathon runners cover a set distance).</li>
  </ul>

  <h4>Why Use Displacement?</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Essential for determining net change in position.</li>
    <li>Used in calculating velocity: <code>v = Δx / t</code>.</li>
  </ul>
</Section>

<Section type="Important Notes">
  <ul>
    <li>Displacement can be zero even if distance is not (e.g., returning to start).</li>
    <li>Displacement cannot be greater than distance — only equal or smaller.</li>
    <li>In one-dimensional motion, direction is indicated by + or − signs.</li>
  </ul>
</Section>

<Section type="Summary">
  <p>
    <strong>Distance</strong> is "how much ground an object has covered" during its motion.  
    <strong>Displacement</strong> is "how far out of place an object is" — the object’s overall change in position.
  </p>
  <p>Think of distance as the odometer reading in a car, while displacement is the straight-line vector from start to finish.</p>
</Section>
